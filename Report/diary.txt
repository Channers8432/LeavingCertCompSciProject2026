Development Diary

Every Sunday, I attend a formal Computer Science session for two hours. As I am doing this subject externally, these sessions are really important for keeping on track with the course and getting guidance on how the project is going.

Also note that this diary was compiled as the project progressed and was updated at random intervals.. Consequently, there may be occasional inconsistencies in tense, tone, or formatting, reflecting the project's status at various points in time.



Week 1

When the project was first announced, I found myself at a crossroads regarding the direction of my project. After discussing the brief with my teacher and classmates, we collectively decided to build a system designed to monitor a living potted plant, which would give us a real physical subject to collect meaningful data from over the course of the project to complete the basic requirements.

We moved immediately into looking into the equipment we would need. I opted for a Raspberry Pi 4B as the central processing unit, paired with a capacitive soil moisture sensor and a BME280 temperature sensor. I specifically chose a capacitive sensor over a resistive one to avoid the corrosion issues common in long-term soil monitoring. I placed an order for the following:
  - Raspberry Pi 4B
  - Capacitive Soil Moisture Sensor V2.0.0
  - BME280 3.3V Atmospheric Pressure/Temp Sensor
  - Breadboard and an assortment of jumper wires
  - MCP3008-I/P (Analogue-to-Digital Converter (ADC), necessary because the Raspberry Pi lacks native analogue input pins)

While awaiting delivery, my teacher provided a retrospective on successful projects from previous years, emphasising the importance of clear documentation and the iterative design process.



Week 2

With the hardware in transit, I spent this week refining my project's focus. The embedded system component was settled, but I still needed to decide what the simulation model would actually model. The brief listed several possible disaster risk scenarios such as, wildfire, drought, pest outbreak, air quality, landslides, and flooding, and also suggested broader themes such as canopy cover modelling, biodiversity changes, and species population dynamics. I gave a few of these some serious thought before deciding which way to go. A drought cycle model was probably the most obvious fit for the soil moisture sensor, where I could track soil dryness over time and model the knock-on effects on tree health and carbon storage. A pest outbreak simulation was another option, using temperature and moisture readings as inputs to a population spread model representing insects moving through a forest grid. An air quality model was also feasible, where the atmospheric sensor data could drive a simulation of particulate dispersal across a grid under different wind conditions. All three were viable and would have satisfied the brief's requirements, but none of them felt particularly engaging to work on for twelve weeks.

I finally decided to base my project on wildfires, after being inspired by the event of the Palisades Fire, which broke out in Los Angeles in January 2025 and became one of the most destructive fires in California's history. Watching that unfold in real time made the topic feel immediate and genuinely important rather than abstract. Beyond that, I have always had a strong interest in emergency services and the systems that support them, and the idea that a simulation tool could realistically assist a fire department in planning evacuations or modelling fire behaviour under different weather conditions made the project feel like it had a practical purpose beyond just fulfilling a brief. That combination of a real-world trigger and a personal interest in the subject made wildfires a clear choice.

After reading the brief again I noticed that Advanced Requirement 2 encourages the use of open-source data, which meant I needed a real, publicly available dataset to work with. While I didn't know its exact application as of now, I felt it was better to secure the data now rather than risk a delay later. I began researching wildfire datasets and came across the Algerian Forest Fires dataset on Kaggle. The main reason I chose it was simply because it had a lot of good, usable data: 244 daily observations across two regions with multiple variables already calculated, including the Initial Spread Index and the Fire Weather Index, which are real indices used by fire services. Most of the other datasets I found were either too sparse, lacked the right variables, or required significant preprocessing before they would be usable. This one was clean enough to work with relatively quickly while still being substantive. I downloaded the CSV and saved it into my artefacts folder.



Week 3

Most of my equipment arrived this week, bar the Raspberry Pi 4B, though with a minor complication: the manufacturer shipped a BMP280 instead of the BME280. Upon checking the datasheets, I found that the BMP280 lacks humidity sensing but handles temperature and pressure identically. Since I planned to use a dedicated soil sensor and hadn't even considered humidity, I decided to proceed with the BMP280 to avoid shipping delays.

I spent the session on hardware assembly and circuit testing. I soldered the header pins onto the BMP280, using a breadboard to hold the pins steady and applying the soldering iron to create clean joints. I then ran a continuity test using a multimeter, probing each pin to verify it had a solid electrical connection to the PCB and that there were no accidental solder bridges between adjacent pins. This confirmed that the BMP280 was correctly prepared before any wiring began. Once I was satisfied with my soldering, I placed wires on each row of the breadboard that had a BMP280 pin in it.

For the wider circuit, I placed the MCP3008 across the centre notch of the breadboard. The chip acts as the translator that allows the Raspberry Pi to understand the analogue signals from the soil sensor, since the Pi has no native analogue input pins of its own. I daisy-chained male-to-female wires together so that the soil sensor could reach a plant while the rest of the system stayed on a desk.



Week 4

While the Pi was still in transit, I shifted focus entirely to building the computer-based simulation model. [AR1, disaster risk model development]

Firstly, I designed a spread probability formula using a sigmoid function to simulate how environmental variables influence the likelihood of a fire jumping between cells. I used temperature and soil moisture as the primary inputs; the probability of spreading is calculated by factoring temperature against the inverse square of soil moisture. This ensures that as soil moisture decreases or temperature increases, the probability rises rapidly. I then applied a constant to increase the overall chance of spreading, using the following formula:
0.15 * (1 / (1 + exp(-(temperature - 25) / 7))) * (1 - soil moisture)^2 * 2

I then began building a basic agent-based model that infected adjacent cells using this formula, incorporating four states: EMPTY, TREE (healthy), BURNING (infected), and BURNT (recovered). I initialised a grid and set the centre cell to BURNING, displaying a line graph via matplotlib once the simulation had finished. The graph tracks three values at each time step: the count of healthy trees in green, active burning cells in orange, and fully burned cells in black, reading from three history lists that step() appends to on every call. This provides a clear picture of the fire's progression over time.

The next iteration involved replacing the static line graph with a grid visualisation rendered as a colourmap. Each cell was assigned a colour based on its state: white for EMPTY, green for TREE, orange for BURNING, and black for BURNT. This made the spatial behaviour of the fire immediately readable in a way the line graph alone could not convey.

To make the temperature behaviour more realistic I replaced the static temperature input with a diurnal cycle. Rather than holding temperature constant throughout the simulation, the model now uses a sine function tied to the internal simulation clock to vary temperature continuously across a 24-hour period. The user-set slider value becomes the daily mean, and the model adds or subtracts up to five degrees depending on the time of day, with the peak occurring at 12:00 and the minimum at 00:00. Each simulation step advances the internal clock by half an hour, so a full simulated day takes 48 steps. This means fire behaviour naturally intensifies during the hottest part of the afternoon and slows overnight as conditions cool, which mirrors real wildfire dynamics. A live temperature reading was inserted to the Live Analytics panel. Wind was the final major addition to the model this week. I added a wind speed slider ranging from zero to ten kilometres per hour and a custom compass widget that the user can click or drag to set wind direction in degrees, with an arrow indicating the current direction visually. Internally the direction is converted to a unit vector at each time step. When evaluating whether a burning cell spreads to each of its eight neighbours, the alignment between the spread direction and the wind vector is calculated using a dot product. Cells that are downwind receive a positive wind multiplier increasing their ignition probability, while cells directly upwind receive a reduced multiplier, with a minimum floor to prevent spread being completely blocked in any direction. At wind speeds above five kilometres per hour the model introduces long-range spotting, where a burning cell has a chance each step of projecting an ember ahead in the wind direction by a random distance of between three and nine cells, which can ignite trees well beyond the main fire front and bypass firebreaks. Above seven kilometres per hour a separate drifting ember system activates, generating particles that travel with the wind over multiple time steps before their lifetime expires, rendered as small star markers on the grid. The live analytics panel also converts the wind direction from degrees into a cardinal compass bearing such as N, NE, or SW, displaying it alongside the speed so it remains readable without the user needing to interpret an angle. Together these wind mechanics produce the elongated fire shapes and unpredictable spot fire behaviour characteristic of real wind-driven wildfires, and the addition of wind as a variable significantly strengthens the what-if scenario capability since changing speed and direction produces dramatically different outcomes from identical starting conditions.

I designed the two scenarios around distinct real-world risk profiles. At the top of each tab I added two buttons to select the spread algorithm: one for the closed-source function and one for the open-source function, both of which were placeholders at this stage. Week 8

With a full week of sensor data collected I turned my attention to analysing it. The dataset included the watering spike from the 9th of March where Moisture_Percent jumped to 93.5%, which was exactly the kind of real sensor response I had tested for in Week 6. Rather than removing it, I kept it in the dataset as a genuine data point, it represented a real change in soil conditions and removing it would have meant discarding valid evidence of the sensor working correctly. I started with the open-source dataset, as the analysis script for that was more straightforward to write first. The Algerian Forest Fires CSV had non-numeric header rows embedded partway through the file, so the cleaning step attempted to parse the first field of every row as an integer using int(row[0]) and skipped any row where that failed. Each row was also split on commas and stripped of surrounding whitespace individually rather than relying on a DictReader, which handled the inconsistent spacing in the raw file. The dataset contained daily weather readings and fire occurrence labels across two Algerian regions during summer 2012, including the Initial Spread Index and the Fire Weather Index for each day, both established indices used in real wildfire management.

Using numpy.polyfit I fitted a linear regression of FWI against ISI directly from the data, obtaining FWI = 1.653 × ISI − 0.804 with an R-squared of 0.852. This was a strong fit, which made sense because FWI is mathematically derived partly from ISI within the Canadian Forest Fire Weather Index system. Colouring the scatter plot by fire and no-fire days made the separation clear: fire days clustered toward higher ISI and FWI values while no-fire days sat mostly at the lower end, with some overlap in the middle. That overlap was informative rather than problematic, since identical weather conditions do not always produce the same outcome in reality, which is precisely why a probabilistic model is more appropriate than a hard threshold.

For the sigmoid that converts FWI into a spread probability, I took the midpoint between the mean FWI of the no-fire days (nofire_fwi) and the mean FWI of the fire days (fire_fwi), which gave a sig_centre of 6.35, and used half the standard deviation of all FWI values as the scale parameter, giving a sig_scale of 3.71. Centring the sigmoid at the point where the two historical populations overlap means the function assigns roughly 50% probability at the boundary between fire and no-fire conditions in the real data, which felt like the most defensible choice. The ISI value fed into the regression is itself calculated from wind speed and fuel dryness using standard FWI system formulae, with fuel dryness approximated from the simulation's soil moisture and temperature values. The constants derived from this script, slope, intercept, sig_centre, and sig_scale, are used directly in the open-source spread function in main.py, where stochastic noise is also applied to the final p_spread value, randomly varying it between 0.8 and 1.2 times the base value each call, to prevent the simulation from being perfectly deterministic and to reflect the natural variability present in the historical data.

With the open-source analysis done I then ran the same process on my own plant_data.csv. I ran a linear regression between temperature and soil moisture percentage using numpy.polyfit. The regression produced slope and intercept values giving the equation Moisture_Percent = -0.2795 × Temp_C + 21.37, confirming a statistically significant negative correlation between temperature and moisture. The direction made physical sense: as temperature rises, the soil dries out faster. The R-squared of 0.177 initially concerned me, but on reflection it was reasonable given the setup. The sensor was indoors in a stable environment and the temperature barely moved across the week, mostly sitting between 8 and 11 degrees Celsius. When the input variable has that narrow a range, sensor noise will always dominate the variation, so a modest R-squared is expected rather than a sign that something went wrong. The p-value coming in well below 0.001 confirmed that the negative trend was statistically genuine.

The regression became the mathematical backbone of the closed-source spread function. Inside the function, the current simulation temperature is passed into the regression equation to predict what moisture level would be expected at that temperature. That predicted value is then blended 50/50 with the user's soil moisture slider, so the real-world data anchors the baseline while the slider remains meaningful for what-if exploration. A dryness effect is then calculated as (1 − pred_moisture)², which amplifies the impact of dry conditions nonlinearly. The temperature effect uses a sigmoid centred on mean_T (9.92°C, the mean of Temp_C from the dataset) with a scale derived from std_T (2.05, its standard deviation), meaning the sigmoid transition is grounded in the actual range of conditions the sensor recorded rather than being an arbitrary choice. The final p_spread is the product of temp_effect and dryness multiplied by a base rate of 0.30.
